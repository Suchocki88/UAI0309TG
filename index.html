Lesson 3.9: Synthesizing Prompt Engineering Skills

Discussion Day | Race for the Chocolate Activity

📋 Lesson Overview

Big Picture

Chapter 3 concludes with a high-energy, collaborative assessment activity that serves triple duty: (1) synthesizing two weeks of prompt engineering learning, (2) identifying knowledge gaps before the Unit B exam, and (3) building team cohesion before entering the potentially sensitive territory of Chapter 4 (Bias).

Learning Objectives

  • Apply conceptual understanding of prompt engineering to challenging scenarios
  • Practice collaborative problem-solving under time pressure
  • Identify areas needing review before Unit B assessment
  • Experience how team discussion deepens individual understanding

Connection to Prior Learning

This activity synthesizes:

  • Lessons 3.1-3.3: Prompt engineering techniques and iteration
  • Lessons 3.4-3.5: Editorial judgment and evaluation
  • Lesson 3.7: RLHF ethics and human labor in AI
  • Lesson 3.8: AI as problem-solving partner

🔧 Teacher Preparation

Before Class (15 minutes setup time)

  • Print and post questions: Use provided 8-page PDF. Post questions around the room in accessible locations. Space them so teams can gather at each without crowding.
  • Prepare answer key: Use provided Scantron-style answer key. Punch out the correct answers so you can quickly overlay it on team submissions.
  • Create answer sheets: Either use blank Scantron forms or the provided template (one per team). Have extras ready.
  • Prepare marking system: Have colored markers ready to mark each team's submission round (prevents re-grading same sheet).
  • Secure prize: Have it ready but not visible yet.

Mental Preparation

This activity works best when you:

  • Set energetic tone: This should feel fun, competitive, intellectually challenging
  • Emphasize learning over winning: Celebrate good thinking, not just right answers
  • Moderate spirited debate: During review, draw out reasoning from multiple perspectives

👨‍🏫 Detailed Facilitation Guide

Opening: Set Up the Challenge (5 minutes)

SUGGESTED OPENING SCRIPT

"Alright, we've spent two weeks on prompt engineering—learning techniques, building editorial judgment, exploring ethics, and using AI as a thinking partner. Today we put it all together in a way that's both fun and serious exam prep.

"You're going to compete in teams on 8 challenging questions. These aren't easy—they're similar to what you'll see on the Unit B exam. But today, you get to work together.

"Here's how it works..."

Explain the Mechanics Clearly

  1. Questions are posted around the room (gesture to them). Your team can tackle them in any order.
  2. Work together to answer all 8. Decide your strategy: split up and divide questions, or move as a team? Your call.
  3. When ready, submit your answer sheet. I'll quickly check it and tell you HOW MANY you got wrong—but not WHICH ones. You'll need to figure that out.
  4. If you miss any, you go back and revise. But you have to wait until all other teams have submitted before you can submit again. This keeps it fair.
  5. First team to get 8/8 wins the prize and becomes teaching assistants—you'll help other teams without giving direct answers.
  6. After we have a winner, we'll review all questions together so everyone understands the reasoning.

💡 Setting Expectations

"These questions are designed to be tough. Getting 6/8 or 7/8 on your first try is actually quite good. The challenge is in the discussion—figuring out WHY an answer is right, not just guessing."

Team Organization

  • Use existing teams if you have them
  • Or: count off to create teams of 3-4 students
  • Give each team an answer sheet
  • Clarify: ONE answer sheet per team with all members' names

Team Competition Phase (25 minutes)

Your Role During Competition

Monitor and Support (But Don't Teach)

  • Circulate quietly to observe team dynamics and discussion quality
  • Answer procedural questions but not content questions
  • Encourage productive debate: "I hear two different answers—keep discussing"
  • Redirect off-task teams gently
  • Note which questions cause most debate for review discussion

Grading Submissions

Quick-Check Process:

  1. Team brings answer sheet to you
  2. Overlay your punched-out answer key
  3. Count number of misses (don't say which ones)
  4. Mark their sheet with colored marker (Round 1, Round 2, etc.)
  5. Announce result: "You got 6 out of 8" or "You have 2 wrong"
  6. If 8/8: Celebrate! They're the winners and become TAs
  7. If wrong: "Back to your team—which ones might it be?"

⚠️ Important: The "Waiting" Rule

Once a team submits and gets feedback, they must wait until ALL other teams have submitted (or until a set time—maybe 3-5 minutes) before they can resubmit. This prevents one team from dominating your attention and gives all teams equal chances.

When First Team Wins (Around 15-20 minutes in)

  • Celebrate enthusiastically: "We have our champions! Let's give them a round of applause!"
  • Award the prize with fanfare
  • Commission them as teaching assistants: "Your job now is to help other teams think through the questions—but don't give away answers. Ask questions, encourage discussion, but let them figure it out."
  • Pull out second-place incentive if you have one: "We still need a second-place team..."

💡 If No Winner After 20 Minutes

Energy may be flagging. Consider:

  • "I'll give you one hint: Question 5 is tricky because..."
  • "Let's do one more round of submissions, then we'll review together"
  • "You've all done excellent work. Let's move to reviewing the questions together"

Don't let it drag to the point of frustration. The review is where the real learning happens.

Whole-Class Review (12 minutes)

TRANSITION TO REVIEW

"Alright, let's go through each question together. Even if your team got it right, you need to understand WHY—because you'll see questions like these on the exam."

For Each Question (1.5 minutes per question):

  1. Read the question aloud (or have a student read it)
  2. Quick poll: "Raise your hand for A... B... C... D..."
  3. Invite reasoning: "Who wants to defend their answer?"
  4. Draw out debate if multiple answers have support
  5. Reveal correct answer and explain reasoning thoroughly
  6. Explain why each distractor is tempting but wrong
  7. Connect to lesson content: "Remember in 3.4 when we talked about..."

✅ Review Goals

Students should leave understanding:

  • Why the correct answer is correct (conceptual reasoning)
  • What misconception each distractor represents
  • How this question connects to their learning
  • What this tells them about the upcoming exam

Prioritize Questions That Caused Most Confusion

If time is tight, spend more time on questions where teams struggled or debated heavily. Breeze through questions most teams got right quickly.

Field Journal Reflection (6 minutes)

TRANSITION TO REFLECTION

"Before we close, take a few minutes to reflect in your field journals. Think about three things: your team's strategy and collaboration, which concepts feel clear versus unclear, and how ready you feel for the exam."

Display the three reflection prompts (from student guide) on board/screen. Circulate quietly while students write.

Closing (2 minutes)

  • Final congratulations to winners
  • Acknowledge all teams' effort: "These were genuinely challenging questions. You all showed strong thinking."
  • Preview Chapter 4: "Next lesson we start exploring bias in AI—how does bias get into these systems?"
  • Exam reminder: "Unit B exam is coming after Chapter 4. Today should help you know what to review."

Quick Reference for Review Discussion:

Question 1: Why "creates surface-level plausibility without deep logical coherence" (B)?
This captures the Lesson 3.8 insight that AI pattern-matches to produce code that LOOKS right but may lack internal consistency. Not about speed (C) or training data quantity (D).

Question 2: Why "specific criteria for what 'interesting' means" (C)?
Vague terms like "interesting" need translation into specific, actionable direction. Core lesson from 3.2-3.3 about editorial judgment guiding prompts.

Question 3: Why "requires judgment about what information is relevant" (C)?
The biomimicry case study showed that effective prompting isn't about rules (more vs. less context) but about JUDGMENT of relevance. Key Lesson 3.3 insight.

Question 4: Why "Editorial judgment—recognizing quality to guide decisions" (B)?
Evaluation IS editorial judgment in action. Lesson 3.5 core concept: recognizing good output is what enables effective direction.

Question 5: Why "Specifying exactly what aspect needs improvement and how" (C)?
"Write this better" is vague. Effective iteration requires SPECIFIC direction about WHAT to improve and HOW. Deep understanding of iteration from Chapter 3.

Question 6: Why "AI cannot learn without humans first recognizing what 'better' means" (A)?
RLHF requires human judgment to define quality—AI can't discover "better" on its own. Connects Lesson 3.7 ethics to fundamental AI limitation.

Question 7: Why "Student A will have stronger results because iteration refines toward quality" (B)?
Process matters beyond initial prompts. Iteration + evaluation drives quality improvement. Core Chapter 3 principle.

Question 8: Why "Evaluate output before spending time on complex new prompts" (B)?
The 30-second critique pattern from Lesson 3.2 emphasizes rapid evaluation to guide next moves efficiently, not speed for speed's sake.

🚧 Anticipated Challenges & Solutions

Challenge 1: One Team Dominates, Others Get Discouraged

In the moment: Emphasize that different teams are strong on different questions. "Team B just nailed Question 5 that Team A struggled with—nice work!"

Prevention: Set tone early that these questions are DESIGNED to be hard. Celebrate good thinking, not just winning.

Challenge 2: Teams Finish Too Quickly (All Wrong)

If a team submits in 5 minutes with many wrong: "You worked quickly—that's one strategy. Now you get to think more deeply about which ones might be wrong. Use this time well."

Redirect: Encourage them to revisit questions with more discussion rather than just guessing again.

Challenge 3: No Team Wins After 20 Minutes

Options:

  • Offer strategic hint on hardest question
  • Extend time by 5 minutes if teams are close
  • Transition to review, declaring "closest team" as winner

Challenge 4: Students Ask for Hints During Competition

Response: "I can't help during competition—that's what makes it fair. But talk it through with your team. What's your reasoning for each answer?"

Challenge 5: Argument About Answer During Review

This is GOOD! "Great debate. Let's hear both sides. Who thinks it's C? Why? Who thinks it's D? Why?" Draw out reasoning, then explain why one is correct and what makes the other tempting.

♿ Differentiation & Support

For Teams Struggling

Challenge Support Strategy
Can't agree on answers • "That's actually good—you're thinking critically. Can you both explain your reasoning?"
• "What evidence from the lessons supports each answer?"
• Encourage debate rather than voting
Guessing randomly • "Slow down. Read the question carefully. What concept is this testing?"
• "Can you eliminate any answers that are clearly wrong?"
• Redirect to strategic thinking
One member dominating • Private word to dominant student: "Make sure everyone's voice is heard"
• To quiet member: "What's your thinking on this one?"

For Individual Students With Needs

Need Accommodation
Reading challenges • Allow student to read questions aloud to themselves
• Pair with supportive teammate who can read aloud
• Focus on concept discussion once question is understood
Social anxiety in teams • Assign specific role: "You're the recorder—write down the team's answers"
• Check in privately: "How's it going with your team?"
• Validate contributions: "Good point you made about Question 3"
ESL students • Pair with patient, supportive teammates
• Allow extra processing time
• Focus on conceptual understanding, not language precision
